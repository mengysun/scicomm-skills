# The script voice — scholars, not sales

The script is the words actually said over each page, and its register matters
as much as its content. The recurring failure mode is the **pitch register**:
prose engineered to impress on the page — punchlines, drama, superlatives —
which sounds *recited* the moment it is spoken to a room of researchers.
Scholars trust understatement, first person, and honest hedging. Write that.

## Write speech, not prose that gets read aloud

The test for every sentence: **would you say it on the phone to a colleague?**
Spoken language is redundant, hedged, and a little loose; that is what makes it
sound like a person. Keep "long story short", "spoiler alert", "maybe of most
interest to this audience" — and keep the speaker's contractions and asides.

| Reads well on the page | Why it fails aloud | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Fragmented. Stale. Duplicated." | one-word drumbeat — sounds rehearsed | say the three problems in one ordinary sentence |
| "The famous figure is downstream of a spreadsheet." | an aphorism built to be quoted | make the point plainly and move on |
| "Question three… Question four…" | symmetric scaffold — recited, not told | vary the transitions; let one example be brief |
| "And here's the part I care about most. *(pause)*" | a scripted money beat | "In addition, we also found that…" |
| "Nine cents versus sixty-six cents." | reading the slide to the room | see the numbers rule below |

## The slide carries the numbers

Exact values are printed on the slide; the audience can read them faster than
they can be said. Speaking the digit string forces the room to choose between
listening and reading — so the mouth carries the **approximation and the
meaning**: "over 90 percent", "a fraction of the cost of human labor",
"perfect accuracy on both". The exact test, the *n*, and the effect size stay
ready for the question — in the script's backup lines, not in the delivery.

## Moves that land in a scholarly room

- **Cite people, warmly, not papers formally.** "One of my favorite works,
  from Pierre Azoulay" persuades more than a symmetric literature recitation.
  Naming why a paper matters to the speaker is evidence, not padding.
- **Anchor on what the audience already owns** — the tools they subscribe to,
  the papers this room cites, the chore they already do. Adoption arguments
  land from inside the listener's stack, not from a feature list.
- **Frame a limitation as its honest affordance, when true.** "The agent can
  tell which entries it failed on, so a human can collect exactly those — this
  enables division of labor" beats the defensive "note that it does not
  hallucinate." Convert the weakness into what the audience can *do* with it —
  never spin; only reframings that are actually true.
- **Plain verdicts.** "The results were really good" is allowed. A scholarly
  room hears engineered enthusiasm as selling.
- **Transitions do the argument.** The sentence that gets from one slide to
  the next is where the logic lives ("This issue turns out to be remarkably
  universal…"). If it cannot be written, the deck order is wrong.
- **Pre-defend nothing.** Meet the expected objection once, at the turn, in
  the open — not as nervous asides scattered through the script.

## Budget honestly

Plan the spoken script at **~130 words per minute**, and aim it at **70–80% of
the slot** — a 13-minute slot wants nine to eleven minutes of script. The
slack absorbs the ad-libs, the pointing, the pauses that real delivery adds,
and leaves room for slides added late. A script that fills the slot to the
brim on paper runs over in the room. No one has ever complained about a talk
ending a minute early.

## Cues

Mark every build advance inline, where the speech expects the change:
`[CLICK — what appears]`. On the PDF path a "click" is the advance to the
build's next page; the marker convention is the same. Before writing a cue's
label, **look at the rendered page** and name what actually appears — never
infer it from the outline (screenshots get misidentified; check, don't guess).

## Polishing a draft the user wrote

When the user has written or edited script text — in chat or in the review
room — **their draft is authoritative**. The job is copy-editing, not
rewriting:

- Fix grammar, agreement, tense, spelling, punctuation, run-ons. Nothing else.
- Keep every idea, every ordering, every aside, every informality, and their
  signature phrases — these are the voice, not noise.
- Add no numbers, no metaphors, no sentences of your own.
- Verify the polish by diff: if a change is not a mechanical correction,
  revert it.
- Where the user's version and yours disagree on substance, assume the user is
  right about their own field and their own audience — check the artifact
  before "correcting" them.
